# v6.0.0a86 — The wheel, read

This alpha is about one thing: what a chart looks like to someone reading it.
No calculation changed. What changed is where the ink sits, how much of it there
is, and — in four places — whether a mark was where it claimed to be at all.

The through-line is that almost everything here was decided by measuring the
drawing rather than by looking at the constants. A browser rasterizes the
symbols and the strings, the pixels are counted, and the number goes into the
code with the reason beside it. Several of the defects below had been shipping
for months precisely because the constants said the right thing while the
drawing did something else.

## Three themes, not six

`light`, `strawberry` and `dark-high-contrast` are gone. They were the three the
accessibility pass never covered, and measuring them says why: against the
surface each mark is actually drawn on, `light` had 66 marks under threshold,
`strawberry` 72, `dark-high-contrast` 19 — while `classic`, `dark` and
`black-and-white` have none.

The worst of them was the one called `light`, which is the first name anyone
tries for a pale chart: its Ascendant sat at **1.51:1**, yellow on white.

What ships: **`classic`** (the light rainbow theme, still the default),
**`dark`**, **`black-and-white`**, and `theme=None` for a drawing that takes its
colours from the document hosting it. Passing a removed name raises
`KerykeionException`, as any unknown name always has.

## Contrast, measured against the right surface

The three surviving themes were re-measured mark by mark, against the ring each
mark is drawn on rather than against the page. Where a point's colour doubles as
text — the degree, the sign, the minutes all take the planet's colour — it is
held to **7:1**, not the 3:1 a graphic object owes.

House cusp lines got a variable of their own, `--kerykeion-modern-cusp`. A
boundary is information, and it was being drawn at the weight of decoration.

Every chart now declares `role="img"`, gives its `<title>` an id, and carries a
`<desc>`: a screen reader announces the subject, the date, the place and the
house system instead of "graphic".

**One trade is stated rather than hidden.** An angle's reading is written across
its own axis line by construction, so "As 19º ♈ 45'" always had a 0.6-wide
stroke through the words. Where a reading crosses a line, the line now drops to
0.35 for exactly the span of that reading — all of it or none, because a line
dimmed under some rows and solid under the others reads as damage. The trigger
is geometric and never "this point is that axis": a planet within a degree of a
cusp covers the line just as squarely, and an angle is not always its own house
cusp.

## Four things that were not where they said they were

**The outer ring of a dual wheel had no visible indicators.** Not missing —
misplaced. Both rings anchored their tether at the boundary between them, which
is the inner ring's outer edge and the outer ring's *inner* edge. Every outer
tether was drawn twelve units from the planet it points at, sitting on the
boundary and pointing outward at nothing, 483 of them per chart. The boundary
then carried two families of identical brackets back to back, which is why the
inner ring's own tether looked as though it pointed the wrong way. It never did.

**The chart angles had a lane that went to the asteroids.** The four angles were
meant to sit further out than the two radii the points alternate between, but
the code that recognised an angle did it by index in a fixed list, and the v6
catalog moved the angles off those indices. For years the outer lane went to
Ceres, Pallas, Juno and Vesta while the angles alternated with everything else.
Repairing the classification by name sent the angles out into the zodiac ring —
so the dedicated lane is removed rather than restored. An angle is a point.

**The lunar disc hung halfway up a tall page.** On a Natal chart taller than the
usual 580 the glyph followed the blank rows *inside* its text block but not the
offset *of* the block, so on a chart with every point active it stayed 500
pixels above the lines it captions.

**A tether ended inside the glyph it points at.** A glyph is drawn upright in an
axis-aligned box while the tether runs along the cluster's radius, and a radius
meeting that box on the diagonal reaches its corner — 2.66 units for the widest
symbol against the 2.15 the row was placed for. On a crowded wheel most clusters
sit near a diagonal.

## The wheel uses the page it is given

A chart with every point active is drawn on a canvas twice the usual height,
because the aspect grid is a pyramid and 52 points make a tall one. The wheel
was not: a fixed 480 across whatever else happened, so it occupied **13%** of
that page — 7% on the largest — with glyphs the same 20 pixels they are on a
chart a quarter the size.

It grows now, and only where there is somewhere to grow. The room is not where
it looks: between the wheel's edge and the planet grid there are 42 pixels,
because the grid is parked at `wheel_right + 20`. So the limit was measured — a
sweep that renders the wheel and everything else separately and counts the
pixels where both are dark:

| canvas | clean to | first contact |
|---|---|---|
| 800–1000 | 1.25 | 1.30 |
| 1000+ | 1.45 | 1.60 |

The shipped figures are 1.15 and 1.45, each about a tenth short of contact, and
72 further cases came back with zero overlapping pixels: both bands, six
languages, three themes, both styles.

Below the gate the scale is exactly 1.0 and no `scale()` is written at all, so
**every ordinary chart is byte for byte what it was.** Dual wheels and charts
with `auto_size=False` never grow: the first has no margin, and the second has
its width frozen while its height is not, so it is already the crowded case.

## The cluster, sized by its ink

- The cusp ring is as thick as the zodiac band beside it: text 1.9, sign glyph
  0.085, three parts 4.0° apart.
- The planet cluster is **12% larger**, spending the room that freed, with the
  air between its rows evened out on the rendered ink rather than on the
  spacing of the anchors.
- The sign glyph is **18% larger** again. The tables said it was already bigger
  than the digits under it; a thin outline beside solid figures reads smaller
  than it measures, and the number that matters is the one a reader sees.
- The aspect web is heavier — line 0.25 → 0.32, the marks that name each aspect
  0.45 → 0.58 — because it is the one part of the wheel read at a glance rather
  than point by point.
- Every row is centred on **its own ink** rather than on its anchor. A
  middle-anchored string centres its advance width while its ink sits about a
  tenth of the font size high, so the digits leaned one way and the glyphs the
  other: the cluster read as a crooked skewer. The spread of row centres goes
  from 0.222 to 0.054.

## The panel reads longest-last

The wheel's chord is what limits those rows, and it stops narrowing them towards
the bottom. So the house system moved down, next to last, and the zodiac line
goes last of all when it carries an ayanamsa — decided from the subject's
zodiac type, not by matching the word, which nine of the ten languages do not
print.

The lunation day is gone. The phase already says where in the cycle the moon is,
in the words a reader thinks in, and the disc beside it says the same in a
picture; a number that has to be translated back into "waxing, about a third of
the way" earns neither the row it took nor the width, which on this panel is the
scarce thing.

A row with nothing to say now takes no slot at all, so a heliocentric chart no
longer opens a blank in the middle of its block.

## Grids that fit their own contents

The reading in a planet grid is anchored at its **end**, so every row hands the
sign glyph the same gap and the figures line up in a column. Anchored at its
start it ran anywhere between 57 and 62.8 depending on how many digits the
degrees needed, and at the wide end it printed its seconds mark across the sign.

A point's name is capped by inked width — ten Latin characters and ten CJK ones
are not the same amount of room — and any trailing marker survives the cut:
`Nordknoten (T)` becomes `Nordkn. (T)`, never `Nordknoten.`, because dropping
the marker would print the true lunar node under the mean node's label. That
alone cleared seven of the ten languages in the overlap sweep.

The comparison tables size their columns from what they actually print, headers
included, instead of from offsets that only ever fitted the English words.

## House numbers that stay with their houses

A quadrant system at high latitude puts four cusps inside three degrees —
Porphyry at 67°N gives houses 10, 11, 12 and 1 a span of 1.139° each. Their
numbers were spread apart by the width of "12" whatever the angle, but a number
is drawn upright while the arc it sits on turns: two at the top of the wheel
meet width to width, two on the flank stack and only their heights meet. They
are spread by their own reach now, so a crowd keeps its ground instead of
marching a house away from the lines it names.

## Notes for anyone building on the SVG

- `kr:cx` / `kr:cy` values change on the charts whose wheel grew. They are
  root-space coordinates and are meant to be read at run time; a consumer that
  reads them from the DOM needs no change, and one that cached them does.
- The `kr:node` vocabulary is unchanged: 59 values before, 59 after, none added
  and none removed.
- A cusp line may now be emitted as more than one `<line>`, with
  `stroke-opacity` on the dimmed span.
- The 338 checked-in SVG baselines were regenerated. They had drifted a long
  way — pre-accessibility markup, the old palette, names the grid no longer
  prints — and went on passing because the comparator allows fifty percent on
  every number and returns silently when the non-numeric skeleton differs.
